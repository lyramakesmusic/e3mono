How E3 mono works

FL Studio's "E3 mono" is zplane elastique Pro V3 in its monophonic mode (internally CelastiqueSOLOV3). It is a time-domain PSOLA. It never touches a spectrum on the output side: it cuts the input into pitch-period grains and lays them back down at a new spacing. This page is a reimplementation from the decompiled FL Studio 20 binary. Against the original DLL it matched sample for sample on every test render (44.1 and 48 kHz, mono and stereo, sweeps and tape stops).

1. Pitch tracking

The input (left + right summed) is resampled to 11025 Hz with a Kaiser-windowed sinc (β = 9, 3 zero crossings), then low-passed by a 2nd-order Butterworth at 4 kHz. Every 128 samples (11.6 ms) it takes a 1024-sample Hann frame and runs an 8192-point FFT.

  • It keeps only the spectral peaks between 25 and 200 Hz (±4 bins around each).
  • It autocorrelates that peak spectrum and adds 0.25 × the normalized spectrum.
  • The f0 candidate is the argmax of a harmonic product: H[i] = 2S[i] · (S[2i]+.15)/2 · (S[3i]+.15)/3. It is refined by a parabola through the neighbouring bins.

Voicing uses a harmonicity in dB (a comb sum over the mean magnitude). A frame is voiced if dB ≥ 3.5, or always if f ≥ 160 Hz. Frames that aren't voiced keep the previous f0, or an older one. Jumps of more than 25% to below 160 Hz without a sharp peak are rejected, so on dense mixes the f0 tends to freeze.

2. Pitch marks

The period is P = sr / f0, capped at 4096. The analysis window is W = clamp(2P, 64, 4096) with half H = W/2. With no f0 yet, it uses W = 512, H = 256.

Each step cross-correlates the window with itself shifted by H:

r[m] = N · Σᵢ x[i+m] · x[i+H]        (FFT, N = next pow2 ≥ 2W)
lag  = argmax floor(r[m] · 10000)     over m ∈ expected ± P/8, r > 0 only
mark = previous mark + H − lag

Voiced marks are then pulled toward the loudest sample (max x²) within ±P/2. They snap if it is within 4 samples; otherwise they move by 2 samples per mark. Marks closer than 8 samples are dropped.

Without a fresh f0, the expected lag is simply the previous lag. So on noisy material the mark spacing drifts by up to ±64 samples per mark.

3. Synthesis

For each output period, take the mark k nearest the input read pointer. Let R = mark[k+1] − mark[k] and let L be the previous output period. With pitch factor p and stretch s:

w(u)  = sin³(πu/2),  u = 0 → 1
a     = min(L, L·p)     before the mark:  out += w · x
b     = min(R, R/p)     after the mark:   out  = (1 − w) · x
                        then zeros up to floor(R/p + 1.5)
next mark    = this mark + floor(R/p)
read pointer += floor(R / (p·s))

Grains keep their original length (that is what preserves formants), and only their spacing changes. Nothing renormalizes the overlap.

4. Why it crunches

At p = 1 neighbouring grains crossfade and sum to 1. Below 1 the grains don't cover the whole output period any more. The envelope sags at every period boundary, so high-frequency content gets chopped into bursts at the output grain rate.

Below p = 0.5 (−12 st) the kernel writes exact digital silence between grains, and each grain becomes an isolated click. On bass, a grain is a couple of cycles of the waveform, so you hear a series of separate blips. When a grain is shorter than a cycle, it's a one-signed bump.

Stretch on its own just repeats or skips grains and barely crunches. Pitch is the knob. A test version that stretches the windows to fill each period (no gaps) is clean again.

"Mono" means monophonic (one pitch at a time), not one channel. Stereo is cut from the L+R analysis, so both channels crack at exactly the same instants. L/R and M/S are not something FL does. They run a separate tracker and set of marks per channel (or for mid and side, decoded back afterwards), which decorrelates the grains.
